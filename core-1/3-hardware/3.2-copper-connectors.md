# Copper Connectors

CompTIA A+ Core 1 — 220-1201  
Objective 3.2 — Cables and Connectors

## What You Need to Know

By the end of this lesson, you should understand:

- RJ11 vs RJ45 connectors and common uses
- What an F-connector is used for
- Why punchdown blocks are used for large cable plants
- Common USB connector styles and USB-C
- What a Molex connector powers
- What Lightning and DB-9 connectors are used for

## What Is It?

Copper connectors are the plugs and jacks that terminate copper cables for networking, power, storage, and peripherals.

This lesson covers:

- RJ11
- RJ45
- F-connectors
- Punchdown blocks
- USB / USB-C
- Molex
- Lightning
- DB-9

## Why Does It Matter?

Technicians must pick the right connector every day:

- DSL modem vs Ethernet port mix-ups
- Cable modem coax terminations
- Terminating large bundles on punchdown blocks
- Powering older drives/fans
- Console access to switches and routers with serial ports

## Real-World Analogy

Think of copper connectors like different keys for different locks.

Most keys will not fit the wrong lock. The dangerous exception is a smaller key that can slide into a larger lock — like RJ11 into an RJ45 port — so you still have to look before you plug.

## How It Works

### RJ11

**RJ11** is a smaller modular connector commonly used for:

- Analog telephone lines
- DSL (Digital Subscriber Line) connections

Details from the lesson:

- Often described as a **6-position** connector with **2 conductors** (**6P2C**)
- Sometimes has four conductors (technically RJ14), but people often still call it RJ11

### RJ45

**RJ45** (Registered Jack type 45) is an **8-position, 8-conductor** modular connector.

Common uses:

- Ethernet networking
- Other uses too, including some serial connections

RJ45 is larger than RJ11. On devices like DSL modems, both ports may sit next to each other.

Important warning:

- An **RJ11 can accidentally fit into an RJ45 port**
- An RJ45 will not fit into an RJ11 port
- Always verify you are plugging into the correct interface

### F-Connector

The **F-connector** is a common coax copper connector used for:

- Cable television
- Cable modem / cable internet connections

Key traits:

- Threaded connector that screws on so it does not pull out easily
- Center copper conductor carries the signal
- Cable modems using **DOCSIS** connect to the cable network through an F-connector

The modem usually has a female F-connector; the coax cable has a male F-connector that screws in.

### Punchdown Blocks

In buildings with hundreds or thousands of twisted-pair cables, wires often terminate on **punchdown blocks**.

Why use them?

- Fast termination
- Relatively inexpensive
- No need to put a connector on every individual wire

Workflow idea:

1. Place wires into the correct positions
2. Use a punchdown tool to seat them
3. Metal contacts pierce insulation and touch the copper inside

### USB Connectors

**USB** (Universal Serial Bus) has several common copper connector styles.

| USB era / style | Connector idea |
| --- | --- |
| USB 1.1 / 2.0 | Standard-A plug; square Standard-B for peripherals; Mini-B or Micro-B on many mobile devices |
| USB 3.0 | A plug similar in size; Standard-B and Micro-B look different from older versions |
| USB-C | One physical connector type that can carry many signal types |

**USB-C** is the physical connector. Signals over it may include USB, Thunderbolt, HDMI, and more.

### Molex

A **Molex** connector is a common **4-pin** internal power connector in older desktops.

Notes from the lesson:

- Named after the Molex Connector Company (one of many Molex-made connectors)
- Also called **AMP Mate-n-Lok**
- Provides power for fans, storage drives, and other internals
- Commonly supplies **12 V** and **5 V**
- Usually connects by friction and is easy to disconnect/reconnect
- Found on older power supplies along with other PSU connectors

### Lightning

**Lightning** is an Apple proprietary connector used with older Apple mobile devices such as iPhones and iPads.

Why Apple used it (from the lesson):

- Extra power capability needs
- Reversible insertion

The industry is moving toward USB-C, but Lightning devices still appear in support work.

### DB-9

**DB-9** connectors commonly carry **serial** communication using **RS-232** (Recommended Standard 232, dating to about 1969).

Older uses:

- Modems
- Mice
- Other serial peripherals

Modern uses for admins/troubleshooters:

- Console ports on switches, routers, firewalls, and similar devices
- Direct command-line configuration over serial

USB has largely replaced everyday serial peripherals, but DB-9 console access is still common in networking gear.

| Term | Meaning |
| --- | --- |
| RJ11 | Small modular connector for phone/DSL |

## Common Mix-Ups

### RJ11 vs RJ45

- RJ11 = phone/DSL, smaller
- RJ45 = usually Ethernet, larger
- RJ11 can fit where it should not — check first

### USB connector vs USB-C signal

USB-C is the shape. The signal may be USB, Thunderbolt, video, or something else.

### Molex vs modern power connectors

Molex is the older 4-pin peripheral power style, not the main ATX motherboard connector.

### Lightning vs USB-C

Lightning is Apple-proprietary and reversible; USB-C is the broader industry direction.
