## What Is It?

When people say “wireless,” they often mean Wi-Fi at home or work. That is only one part of the story.

This lesson covers several wireless technologies used every day:

- 802.11 / Wi-Fi
- Bluetooth
- RFID
- NFC

Each one solves a different problem.

## Why Does It Matter?

Help desk technicians constantly support wireless issues:

- A laptop cannot connect to Wi-Fi
- A headset will not pair over Bluetooth
- A badge will not unlock a door
- A phone payment fails at checkout
- A user asks whether 2.4 GHz or 5 GHz is better

Understanding the technology helps you ask better questions and recognize CompTIA-style scenarios.

## Real-World Analogy

Think of wireless technologies like different kinds of conversation:

- Wi-Fi is like talking across a room so many people can join the same conversation
- Bluetooth is like talking quietly to one nearby person
- RFID is like scanning a name tag
- NFC is like tapping two devices together for a short, close exchange

Same idea of wireless communication. Different distance, purpose, and use cases.

## How It Works

### IEEE 802.11 and Wi-Fi

Wi-Fi networks are standardized by the IEEE, the Institute of Electrical and Electronics Engineers.

The committee that handles these standards is the **802.11** committee. That is why these networks are often called **802.11 networks**.

Older naming used letters such as:

- 802.11ac
- 802.11ax
- 802.11be

Those names can be confusing, so newer marketing names are also used:

| Older / Standard Name | Easier Name |
| --- | --- |
| 802.11ac | Wi-Fi 5 |
| 802.11ax | Wi-Fi 6 / Wi-Fi 6E |
| 802.11be | Wi-Fi 7 |

Memory tip: Wi-Fi 5, Wi-Fi 6, and Wi-Fi 7 are easier labels for newer 802.11 versions.

### Wi-Fi Frequencies

Wi-Fi commonly uses these frequency ranges:

- 2.4 GHz
- 5 GHz
- 6 GHz

Some access points and devices can use more than one range at the same time.

### Channels Make Frequencies Easier

Instead of memorizing exact frequency values, 802.11 groups frequencies into **channels**.

Channels are easier to talk about than exact gigahertz values.

Example from a device configuration:

| Setting | Example |
| --- | --- |
| 2.4 GHz channel | Channel 6 |
| Exact frequency for channel 6 | 2.437 GHz |
| 5 GHz channel | Channel 44 |
| Exact frequency for channel 44 | 5.220 GHz |

It is much easier to say “channel 44” than “5.220 GHz.”

### Bandwidth

Bandwidth here means how much spectrum a communication uses.

Common channel bandwidths include:

- 20 MHz
- 40 MHz
- 80 MHz
- 160 MHz

Wider bandwidth can support higher throughput, but it also uses more of the available spectrum.

### Comparing Frequency Ranges

#### 2.4 GHz

2.4 GHz does not give you many clean options.

In a crowded area, interference is common. With 20 MHz channels, there are really about three non-overlapping channels to choose from.

That is one reason networks expanded into higher ranges.

#### 5 GHz

5 GHz offers more available frequencies.

It can support:

- 20 MHz
- 40 MHz
- 80 MHz
- 160 MHz

More spectrum usually means more room and less crowding than 2.4 GHz.

#### 6 GHz

6 GHz provides even more spectrum to work with.

That gives more room for different channel widths and more flexibility for modern Wi-Fi.

```text
2.4 GHz  → fewer clean channels, more interference risk
5 GHz    → more spectrum, more bandwidth options
6 GHz    → even more spectrum and flexibility
```

### Bluetooth

Bluetooth is another wireless technology. It is common for:

- Wireless headsets
- Wireless speakers
- Other personal accessories

Bluetooth uses the **2.4 GHz** range.

This range is also part of the unlicensed **ISM** band.

ISM stands for Industrial, Scientific, and Medical.

ISM frequencies do not require specialized licenses, which is why everyday devices can use 802.11 and Bluetooth freely.

### Bluetooth Range

Wi-Fi can cover a larger area, such as a building or office floor.

Bluetooth ranges are more limited. For many consumer devices, the maximum range is about **10 meters**.

That is why Bluetooth is often used for devices kept close to the user.

### RFID

RFID stands for Radio Frequency Identification.

Common uses include:

- Building access badges
- Product tags
- Assembly line tracking
- Pet microchips

RFID tags come in different shapes and sizes. Some are tiny, about the size of a grain of rice. Others are flat tags or cards with an antenna around the outside and a chip in the middle.

### Passive RFID Tags

Many RFID tags have **no battery**.

They sit idle with no power until a scanner comes close. The radio energy from the scanner powers the tag long enough for it to send back an ID code.

The scanner then checks that ID against a database.

Some RFID tags do include batteries. Those active tags can often be scanned from a longer distance.

### NFC

NFC stands for Near Field Communication.

NFC builds on RFID-style ideas, but it supports **two-way communication**.

RFID is generally one-way. NFC can work both ways between compatible devices.

Common NFC uses include:

- Paying with a phone or smartwatch at a point-of-sale terminal
- Helping set up Bluetooth or Wi-Fi connections by sharing configuration details
- Opening a door or providing identification with a phone

NFC works over a very short distance, which is why you usually tap or hold devices close together.

## Common Mix-Ups

### 802.11 Letter Names vs Wi-Fi Names

- 802.11ac = Wi-Fi 5
- 802.11ax = Wi-Fi 6 / Wi-Fi 6E
- 802.11be = Wi-Fi 7

### Frequency vs Channel

- Frequency is the actual radio value
- Channel is the easier label used in configuration screens

### Bluetooth vs Wi-Fi

- Wi-Fi is for network and internet access over a larger area
- Bluetooth is usually for nearby personal devices

### RFID vs NFC

- RFID is generally one-way identification
- NFC supports two-way communication and is used for close-range actions like payments and pairing help

### Passive vs Active RFID

- Passive = no battery; powered by the scanner
- Active = has a battery; can often work at longer range
