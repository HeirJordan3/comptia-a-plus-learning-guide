# Internet Connection Types

CompTIA A+ Core 1 — 220-1201  
Objective 2.7 — Network Connections

## What You Need to Know

By the end of this lesson, you should understand:

- Satellite internet basics, latency, line of sight, and rain fade
- Fiber optic internet strengths and trade-offs
- Broadband cable / DOCSIS
- DSL / ADSL and distance limits
- Cellular internet, tethering, and mobile hotspots
- WISP connections

## What Is It?

There are many ways to connect to the internet.

This lesson covers common connection types:

- Satellite
- Fiber
- Broadband cable
- DSL
- Cellular
- WISP

Each option has different speed, cost, distance, and reliability trade-offs.

## Why Does It Matter?

Help desk technicians often hear:

- “My internet is slow.”
- “Video calls feel delayed.”
- “We are too far from town for cable.”
- “Can I share my phone’s internet?”

Knowing connection types helps you explain symptoms like high latency, asymmetric speeds, and remote-site options.

## Real-World Analogy

Think of getting water to a house:

- Fiber = a large high-pressure main
- Cable = a shared utility pipe already in the neighborhood
- DSL = using the existing phone line for extra service
- Satellite = trucking water in from far away
- Cellular / WISP = a local wireless delivery route when pipes are not available

## How It Works

### Satellite

Satellite networking sends signals from Earth to a satellite and back down again.

Pros:

- Useful for remote sites
- Works where traditional wired internet is unavailable

Trade-offs:

- Often more expensive than terrestrial options
- Higher latency because of the long path to space and back

Typical idea from the lesson:

- Around 100 Mbps down / 5 Mbps up is common on many satellite links
- Traditional satellite latency can be about 0.25 seconds up and 0.25 seconds down, or roughly half a second total
- Newer low-Earth systems such as Starlink advertise lower latency, often around 25–60 ms

Other satellite concerns:

- Needs line of sight to the satellite
- Storms can cause **rain fade**

### Fiber

Fiber optics send data as light through a small fiber strand.

Strengths:

- Very high data capacity
- Excellent for long distances compared with copper
- Common for WANs and metro connections
- Also available as fiber to the home in many places

Trade-offs:

- Fiber and fiber equipment are often more expensive than copper
- Fiber repairs can cost more than copper repairs

Homes may convert outdoor fiber to indoor copper connections at the premises.

### Broadband Cable

Cable internet often uses the same coax infrastructure associated with cable television.

This is a **broadband** technology, meaning many frequencies and services can run at the same time over one cable. That can include:

- Data
- Video
- Voice

The data standard is **DOCSIS** (Data Over Cable Service Interface Specification). Different DOCSIS versions support different service levels and speeds.

Common cable internet speeds range from about 50 Mbps to 1 Gbps or higher.

A cable modem usually presents Ethernet for the home or office network.

### DSL

**DSL** stands for Digital Subscriber Line.

It uses existing telephone lines to carry digital data along with voice. You may also hear **ADSL**, Asymmetric Digital Subscriber Line.

**Asymmetric** means download is usually much faster than upload.

Example idea from the lesson:

- About 200 Mbps down / 20 Mbps up is possible on many DSL links

Distance matters:

- Farther from the central office usually means slower speeds
- Rough practical limit is about **10,000 feet** from the central office

### Cellular

Cellular internet uses the same cell-based mobile infrastructure as phones.

Two common sharing methods:

| Method | Meaning |
| --- | --- |
| Tethering | One device uses the phone for internet |
| Mobile hotspot | The phone shares internet with multiple devices |

Carrier plans may allow these features or charge extra, so users should confirm with the provider.

### WISP

A **WISP** is a Wireless Internet Service Provider.

It is useful when cable or phone-line service is hard to get, especially in remote areas.

Setup is often simple: an outdoor antenna connects to the WISP network.

Possible wireless technologies include:

- Meshed 802.11
- 5G home internet
- Other proprietary wireless links

Typical WISP speeds may range from about 10 Mbps to 1,000 Mbps.

## Common Mix-Ups

### Satellite vs Fiber

- Satellite = long wireless path through space; higher latency risk
- Fiber = light through cable; high capacity and long terrestrial distance

### Cable vs DSL

- Cable = coax / DOCSIS / cable provider
- DSL = telephone line / distance to central office matters

### Tethering vs Hotspot

- Tethering = one-to-one
- Hotspot = one-to-many

### WISP vs Home Wi-Fi

- Home Wi-Fi = local wireless inside a building
- WISP = wireless connection to an internet provider, often with an outdoor antenna
