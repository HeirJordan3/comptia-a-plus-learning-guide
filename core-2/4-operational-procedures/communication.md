# Communication

CompTIA A+ Core 2 — 220-1202  
Objective 4.7 — Given a scenario, use proper communication techniques and professionalism

## What You Need to Know

By the end of this lesson, you should understand:

- Why communication skills are as important as technical skills
- Avoiding jargon and acronyms with customers
- Maintaining a **positive attitude** and partnering as equals
- Listening more than interrupting
- Asking probing questions and **repeating back** what you heard
- Setting expectations (options, pros/cons)
- Documenting with follow-up email
- Regular check-ins on long issues and after ticket close

## What Is It?

**Communication** in IT support is translating technical reality into plain language, listening fully, setting clear expectations, and keeping the customer informed until the issue is truly resolved.

## Why Does It Matter?

People often fail tickets on communication, not on the fix. Strong communicators:

- Move up faster in IT careers
- Build confidence and trust
- Collect better details for troubleshooting
- Prevent “forgotten ticket” frustration
- Catch leftover issues after close

## Real-World Analogy

Think of a doctor’s visit:

| Communication skill | Doctor equivalent |
| --- | --- |
| No jargon | “Broken bone,” not Latin first |
| Listen fully | Don’t interrupt the symptom story |
| Repeat back | “So the pain started after you fell?” |
| Options + expectations | Cast vs surgery — pros/cons |
| Follow-up | Call when labs are delayed |
| After “resolved” | “Is anything still bothering you?” |

## How It Works

### Communication Beats “Just Being Technical”

OS, security, and networking knowledge matter — but weak communication is where many techs struggle. Leaders make business decisions from clear explanations, not acronym soup. Put yourself in a non-technical person’s shoes.

### Avoid Jargon With Customers

| Audience | Language |
| --- | --- |
| Other technologists | Acronyms and shortcuts are fine |
| Customers / third parties / leadership | Plain language — **translate** tech into their world |

Goal: be the bridge between the technical world and their workplace. Easy fix: explain it as if the listener never worked in IT.

### Positive Attitude

Attitude is infectious. Positive attitude → better room energy and customer experience.

| Do | Why |
| --- | --- |
| Partner as an equal | Builds confidence; shows you will do the work |
| Stay positive on hard news | Failed drive with no recovery — still offer paths forward |
| Remember repeat contact | You may work with the same people for weeks or months |

### Listen More Than You Talk

Habit: ask questions and listen more than you interrupt.

| Problem | Better habit |
| --- | --- |
| Interrupting before the key point | Let them finish the story |
| Assuming you already know | You don’t know until you’ve heard it all |
| Especially on phone / conference calls | Make sure they feel heard; capture details |

Know when to listen and when to speak — troubleshooting goes smoother.

### Questions, Notes, and Repeat-Back

Don’t assume — **probe**.

1. Listen to the full description  
2. Take notes  
3. **Repeat back** what you heard  
4. Let them correct anything you got wrong  
5. Ask clarifying questions even if you think you know the fix  

What you first believed is often different from reality.

### Set Expectations — Options Are Reality

“Expectation is reality” on technical problems.

| Practice | Example |
| --- | --- |
| Offer options | Repair vs replace |
| Explain both sides | Advantages and disadvantages of each |
| Follow phone talks with email | Same page; problem documented for both parties |

### Stay Visible on Long Issues

Parts or vendor delays can last weeks or months.

| Habit | Effect |
| --- | --- |
| Calendar reminder (e.g. weekly contact) | Customer knows you haven’t forgotten them |
| Update even with “no news yet” | Shows ownership |

### After You Close the Ticket

Check in: was the problem resolved to their satisfaction?

- Often: “Nothing else” — move on  
- Sometimes: one small leftover that would have slipped through — easy to finish to 100%

## Side-by-Side Comparison

| Situation | Strong communication |
| --- | --- |
| Exec asks why the server is down | Plain language; no alphabet soup |
| User mid-story | Don’t interrupt; take notes |
| You think it’s DNS | Ask clarifying questions anyway |
| Repair or replace? | Present both options + tradeoffs |
| Waiting on a part for two weeks | Weekly check-in email/call |
| Ticket closed yesterday | Confirm they’re fully satisfied |

## Key Terms

| Term | Meaning |
| --- | --- |
| Jargon / slang | Tech abbreviations that confuse non-IT listeners |
| Translator role | Explaining tech in business/user terms |
| Active listening | Hear the full story before diagnosing |
| Repeat-back | Restate the issue to confirm understanding |
| Clarifying questions | Probes that test your assumptions |
| Expectation setting | Options, timelines, and documented agreements |
| Follow-up | Status contact during and after resolution |

## Common Mix-Ups

### Technical skill alone is enough

Communication is marketable and often the real gap.

### Acronyms make you sound smarter to customers

They often create confusion — save them for tech peers.

### Interrupting saves time

It often wastes time by missing the real issue.

### “No update from the vendor” means silence to the customer

Still check in — ownership matters.
